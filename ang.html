Solving Trigonometry Equations (Unit Circle Method)

The diagram below shows a circle is the unit circle. The unit circle means the circle with radius 1. The equation for the unit circle is \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\).

\(P(x,y)\) means a general point that lies on the circle and the coordinates of that point are \(x\) and \(y\).

To solve what \(x\) and \(y\) are for \(P(x,y)\), you only need to know the angle. Then you can use \(x=\cos(A)\) and \(y=\sin(A)\), where \(A\) is the angle.

For example, if the angle \(A=30°\), then \(x=\cos(30°)=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\) and \(y=\sin(30°)=\frac{1}{2}\). Then:

\[P(x,y) = (\cos(30°),\sin(30°)) = \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right)\]

The diagram shows all the \(P(x,y)\) for all the special angles.

How To Remember

In general, the x and y coordinates of points that are on opposite sides of the circle to each other are the opposite sign.

The coordinates of angle \(180°\) are the negative of the coordinates of angle \(0°\). For angle \(0°\), \(P(x,y)=(1,0)\). For angle \(180°\), \(P(x,y)=(-1,0)\).

Angle \(270°\) is the negative of angle \(90°\). For angle \(90°\), \(P(x,y)=(0,1)\). For angle \(270°\), \(P(x,y)=(0,-1)\).

For all the other special angles, the denominator of the fractions is \(2\), so you only need to remember the pattern of numerators.

The numerators are \(\sqrt{3}\), \(\sqrt{2}\), or \(1\).

Look at angles \(30°\), \(45°\), and \(60°\): the numerators for the \(x\)-coordinate decrease in order \(\sqrt{3} \rightarrow \sqrt{2} \rightarrow 1\).

Now look at angles \(120°\), \(135°\), \(150°\): the \(x\)-numerators increase \(1 \rightarrow \sqrt{2} \rightarrow \sqrt{3}\).

Then for \(210°\), \(225°\), \(240°\) they decrease again, and for \(300°\), \(315°\), \(330°\) they increase again.

The \(y\)-coordinate numerators do the opposite pattern: when \(x\)-numerators increase, \(y\)-numerators decrease, and vice versa.

For angles \(30°\), \(45°\), \(60°\), both \(x\) and \(y\) are positive. The opposite angles \(210°\), \(225°\), \(240°\) have both negative.

For angles \(120°\), \(135°\), \(150°\), \(x\) is negative and \(y\) is positive. The opposite angles \(300°\), \(315°\), \(330°\) have \(x\) positive and \(y\) negative.

How To Use The Diagram To Solve Trig Equations

Say we have \(2\sin(x) = -1\). Rearrange to get the trig function on its own: divide both sides by 2 to get \(\sin(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\).
We need to find both angles where this is true.
Remember that for \(P(x,y)\), \(x=\cos(A)\) and \(y=\sin(A)\). Since this is a sine equation, focus on the \(y\)-coordinate.
We need angles where \(y=-\frac{1}{2}\). On the diagram that happens at \(x=210°\) or \(x=330°\).

\[\sin(x)=-\frac{1}{2} \text{ when } x=210° \text{ or } x=330°\]

Relation to Trig Graphs

The trig graphs are created directly from the unit circle diagram above. For every angle \(A\) going all the way round the circle from \(0°\) up to (but not including) \(360°\), you calculate the coordinates of \(P(x,y)\) and plot them.

For the \(\sin(A)\) graph, the horizontal axis is the angle \(A\) and the vertical axis is the \(y\)-coordinate of \(P(x,y)\). In other words, to draw the graph for \(\sin(A)\),for each angle you ask: what is the vertical distance from the x axis to the point \(P\)? This distance is then plotted on the \(\sin(A)\) graph. At \(0°\) the point is at \(y=0\), it rises to \(y=1\) at \(90°\), falls back to \(y=0\) at \(180°\), drops to \(y=-1\) at \(270°\), and returns to \(y=0\) at \(360°\) — which gives you the familiar wave shape.

For the \(\cos(A)\) graph, the horizontal axis is still the angle \(A\), but now the vertical axis is the \(x\)-coordinate of \(P(x,y)\). To draw the \(\cos(A)\) graph, for each angle you ask: what is the horizontal distance from the y axis to the point \(P\)? This distance is then plotted on the \(\cos(A)\) graph. At \(0°\) the point is at \(x=1\), it falls to \(x=0\) at \(90°\), reaches \(x=-1\) at \(180°\), rises back through \(x=0\) at \(270°\), and returns to \(x=1\) at \(360°\). This is the same wave shape as sine, just shifted \(90°\) to the left. This happens because the circle is symetrical.

For the \(\tan(A)\) graph, you calculate \(\tan(A) = \dfrac{\sin(A)}{\cos(A)}\) for every angle. Because \(\cos(A)=0\) at \(90°\) and \(270°\), the value of \(\tan(A)\) is undefined there and the graph has vertical asymptotes at those angles.

This is why the three graphs look the way they do — they are not separate things to memorise. They all come from reading off the coordinates of \(P(x,y)\) as the angle goes round the circle.

Question 10 (Higher Challenge)

Solve \(2\sin x+\cos x=0\) for \(0° \le x \le 360°\).

Rearrange: \(2\sin x=-\cos x\Rightarrow \tan x=-\frac{1}{2}\).
For tangent, recall \(\tan(A)=\frac{y}{x}\) for \(P(x,y)\). We need angles where \(\frac{y}{x}=-\frac{1}{2}\).
This value does not appear exactly on the special-angle diagram, so use a calculator: \(\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\approx 26.6°\). We need \(\frac{y}{x}\) to be negative, which happens where \(x\) and \(y\) have opposite signs — on the diagram those are the angles between \(90°\) and \(180°\), and between \(270°\) and \(360°\).
So the two angles are \(180°-26.6°=153.4°\) and \(360°-26.6°=333.4°\).

\[x\approx 153.4°,\ 333.4°\]

Exam Question (Ratios)

Here is the exam question image:

Here is why it works

Let:

\[c = \text{cost of 1 kg of carrots (in pence)}\]

\[t = \text{cost of 1 kg of tomatoes (in pence)}\]

From the question:

\[7c + 5t = 480\]

The ratio \(c:t = 5:9\) means:

\[\frac{t}{c} = \frac{9}{5}\]

Cross multiply:

\[9c = 5t\]

Now substitute into the first equation.

Since \(5t=9c\), replace \(5t\) in \(7c+5t=480\) with \(9c\):

\[7c + 9c = 480\]

\[16c = 480\]

\[c = 30\]

Now use \(9c=5t\):

\[9(30)=5t\]

\[270=5t\]

\[t=54\]

Final answer:

\[\text{Carrots} = 30\text{p per kg}\]

\[\text{Tomatoes} = 54\text{p per kg}\]

Worked Explanation

Since the circle has centre \((0,0)\), any point on the circle is the same distance from the origin.

Point \(P=(5,10)\) is on the circle, so first find the radius squared:

\[r^2 = 5^2 + 10^2\]

\[r^2 = 25 + 100 = 125\]

So the equation of the circle is:

\[x^2 + y^2 = 125\]

Point \(Q\) is also on the circle and has \(x=-2\).

Substitute into the circle equation:

\[(-2)^2 + y^2 = 125\]

\[4 + y^2 = 125\]

\[y^2 = 121\]

\[y = \pm 11\]

So \(Q\) could be:

\[(-2,11)\text{ or }(-2,-11)\]

Now use the information that line \(L\) has a positive gradient. Test both possibilities with \(P=(5,10)\).

Option 1: \(Q=(-2,11)\)
Gradient: \[\frac{10-11}{5-(-2)}=\frac{-1}{7}\] Negative gradient, so reject.
Option 2: \(Q=(-2,-11)\)
Gradient: \[\frac{10-(-11)}{5-(-2)}=\frac{21}{7}=3\] Positive gradient, so this is correct.

So:

\[Q=(-2,-11)\]

Find the equation of the line through \(P=(5,10)\) with gradient \(3\).

Use:

\[y=3x+k\]

Substitute \(P\):

\[10=3(5)+k\]

\[10=15+k\]

\[k=-5\]

Answer:

\[\boxed{k=-5}\]

Finding Coordinates on a Straight Line

This topic is about finding a missing point when you already know two points on the same straight line. We can solve these questions with vectors, and we can also solve many of them using straight-line formulas.

Method 1: Vector Approach (Best for Point-to-Point Movement)

Let the points be:

\[A(x_1,y_1),\quad B(x_2,y_2)\]

The vector from \(A\) to \(B\) is:

\[\overrightarrow{AB}=(x_2-x_1,\ y_2-y_1)\]

Geometrically, every point on the same line through \(A\) is found by moving from \(A\) in exactly the same direction as \(\overrightarrow{AB}\), just by a different amount.

That is why any point \(P\) on this line can be written as:

\[P=A+t\overrightarrow{AB}\]

The parameter \(t\) does not create a new direction; it only scales the same direction vector. So this formula encodes the geometry of one straight line through \(A\) and \(B\).

If \(0<t<1\), then \(P\) is between \(A\) and \(B\). If \(t>1\), it is beyond \(B\). If \(t<0\), it is on the opposite side of \(A\).

Method 2: Straight Line Formula

The vector picture above and the line-equation picture are the same geometry written differently. If \(P(x,y)\) is on the line through \(A(x_1,y_1)\), then the movement from \(A\) to \(P\) is:

\[(x-x_1,\ y-y_1)\]

For points on one straight line, this movement must be parallel to \(\overrightarrow{AB}=(x_2-x_1,\ y_2-y_1)\), so their component ratio is equal.

\[m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}\]

Rearranging that parallel-ratio idea gives point-slope form:

\[y-y_1=m(x-x_1)\]

So this is not a random formula. It is the algebra version of: "the horizontal and vertical movement from \(A\) to any point on the line keep the same ratio."

Worked Examples

Example 1: Midpoint of Two Points

Find the midpoint of \(A(2,5)\) and \(B(10,1)\).

Geometrically, the midpoint is the point reached by taking exactly half of the vector from \(A\) to \(B\).
\(\overrightarrow{AB}=(10-2,\ 1-5)=(8,-4)\), so half of that movement is \((4,-2)\).
Start at \(A\) and add half the movement: \((2,5)+(4,-2)=(6,3)\).
This is equivalent to averaging coordinates, because "halfway" means half in both horizontal and vertical directions.

\[M=\left(\frac{2+10}{2},\frac{5+1}{2}\right)=(6,3)\]

Example 2: Point One Quarter of the Way from A to B

Given \(A(-3,2)\), \(B(5,14)\), find \(P\) where \(P=A+\frac{1}{4}\overrightarrow{AB}\).

The phrase "one quarter of the way from \(A\) to \(B\)" is geometric information about position on the segment \(AB\). So we should use one quarter of the direction vector from \(A\) to \(B\).
\(\overrightarrow{AB}=(5-(-3),\ 14-2)=(8,12)\).
\(\frac{1}{4}\overrightarrow{AB}=(2,3)\).
Add this to \(A\): \(P=A+(2,3)=(-3+2,\ 2+3)=(-1,5)\). This keeps \(P\) on the same line and places it 25% along from \(A\).

\[\boxed{P=(-1,5)}\]

Example 3: Find Missing y using the Line Equation

Points \(A(1,-2)\) and \(B(9,6)\) lie on a line. Find the point on this line where \(x=7\).

Why use a line equation here? Because we are told the unknown point is on the same line as \(A\) and \(B\), and an equation is the algebraic description of that exact geometric set of points.
Build that line from the two known points: \(m=\frac{6-(-2)}{9-1}=1\), then \(y-(-2)=1(x-1)\Rightarrow y=x-3\).
Now enforce the extra geometric condition "the point has \(x=7\)" by substituting into the line equation: \(y=7-3=4\).
So \((7,4)\) is the unique point that satisfies both conditions: it lies on the line and has \(x=7\).

\[\boxed{(7,4)}\]

Example 4: Internal Division in a Ratio

Find point \(P\) dividing \(AB\) in the ratio \(AP:PB=2:3\), where \(A(-4,7)\), \(B(11,-8)\).

The ratio tells us where \(P\) sits on the segment \(AB\). A ratio \(2:3\) means the whole segment is split into \(2+3=5\) equal pieces, and \(P\) is 2 pieces from \(A\).
So geometrically, \(\overrightarrow{AP}=\frac{2}{5}\overrightarrow{AB}\). First compute \(\overrightarrow{AB}=(11-(-4),\ -8-7)=(15,-15)\).
Then \(\overrightarrow{AP}=\frac{2}{5}(15,-15)=(6,-6)\).
Add this move to \(A\): \(P=A+\overrightarrow{AP}=(-4,7)+(6,-6)=(2,1)\).
This matches the geometry: \(P\) is inside the segment and closer to \(A\), since the first ratio part is smaller.

\[\boxed{P=(2,1)}\]

Plans and Elevations

We will think about drawing plans and elevations in terms of faces, edges, and corners. This keeps the drawing clear and stops us from missing important lines.

Step 1: Work out the object dimensions first.

Identify the width, depth, and height of the full 3D object. These three measurements describe the size of the shape before you choose a view.

Step 2: Decide which dimensions matter for the view you are drawing.

For a plan (top view), use width and length. For a front elevation, use width and height. For a side elevation, use length and height.

How To Draw Accurately

First draw the outline of the required plan or elevation. This is the outside boundary of what can be seen in that view.
Then look for places where the shape changes width or height abruptly. Those sudden changes create edges, so draw those edges on.
If an abrupt change creates an edge that is hidden from view, draw that edge as a dotted line. Dotted lines show real edges that exist on the shape but cannot be seen directly from that viewpoint.
Quick check before finishing: have you shown the correct outside outline first, then all visible abrupt edges, and finally any hidden abrupt edges as dotted lines?

Composite and Inverse Functions

7a) Composite Functions

A composite function means applying one function and then feeding the result straight into another. The notation \(fg(x)\) means apply \(g\) first, then apply \(f\) to the result:

\[fg(x) = f\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\]

Order matters: \(fg(x)\) and \(gf(x)\) are usually different. Read right-to-left — the function closest to \(x\) is applied first.

Example 1 — Find \(fg(x)\) and \(gf(x)\)

Let \(f(x) = 2x + 1\) and \(g(x) = x^2\).

\(fg(x)\): apply \(g\) first, then \(f\).
\(fg(x) = f\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = f(x^2) = 2x^2 + 1\)
\(gf(x)\): apply \(f\) first, then \(g\).
\(gf(x) = g\bigl(f(x)\bigr) = g(2x+1) = (2x+1)^2\)

\[\boxed{fg(x) = 2x^2 + 1, \qquad gf(x) = (2x+1)^2}\]

Example 2 — Evaluate a composite function numerically

Let \(f(x) = x + 5\) and \(g(x) = 2x\). Find \(fg(3)\).

Work from the inside out. First find \(g(3)\): \[g(3) = 2 \times 3 = 6\]
Now apply \(f\) to that result: \[f(6) = 6 + 5 = 11\]

\[\boxed{fg(3) = 11}\]

Example 3 — Expand and simplify a composite function

Let \(f(x) = 3x - 2\) and \(g(x) = x + 4\). Find \(gf(x)\) in simplified form.

Apply \(f\) first: \(f(x) = 3x - 2\)
Apply \(g\) to the result: \(g(3x-2) = (3x-2) + 4 = 3x + 2\)

\[\boxed{gf(x) = 3x + 2}\]

7b) Inverse Functions

The inverse function \(f^{-1}(x)\) undoes \(f\). If \(f\) maps \(a\) to \(b\), then \(f^{-1}\) maps \(b\) back to \(a\).

Method to find \(f^{-1}(x)\):

  1. Write \(y = f(x)\).
  2. Rearrange to make \(x\) the subject.
  3. Replace every \(y\) with \(x\) in your answer — that expression is \(f^{-1}(x)\).
Check: \(f\bigl(f^{-1}(x)\bigr) = x\) always. Substituting your answer back into \(f\) should give you \(x\) if it is correct.

Example 4 — Find the inverse of \(f(x) = 3x - 2\)

Write \(y = 3x - 2\).
Rearrange for \(x\): \(y + 2 = 3x \Rightarrow x = \dfrac{y+2}{3}\)
Replace \(y\) with \(x\):

\[\boxed{f^{-1}(x) = \frac{x+2}{3}}\]

Check: \(f\!\left(\dfrac{x+2}{3}\right) = 3 \cdot \dfrac{x+2}{3} - 2 = x + 2 - 2 = x\) ✓

Example 5 — Find the inverse of \(f(x) = \dfrac{2x + 1}{3}\)

Write \(y = \dfrac{2x+1}{3}\).
Multiply both sides by 3: \(3y = 2x + 1\)
Rearrange for \(x\): \(2x = 3y - 1 \Rightarrow x = \dfrac{3y - 1}{2}\)
Replace \(y\) with \(x\):

\[\boxed{f^{-1}(x) = \frac{3x-1}{2}}\]

Rearranging Formulas with Factorisation and Index Rules

These questions need more than one skill. You often need to factorise first, simplify, and then rearrange. For index questions, name the exact index rule each time you use it.

Factorising patterns:

\[ax^2 + bx + c\text{ (try factorising or completing square)}\]

\[x^2 - a^2 = (x-a)(x+a)\]

Index rules we will use:

\[x^{-n} = \frac{1}{x^n}\]

\[(x^m)^n = x^{mn}\]

\[\left(x^m\right)^{\frac{1}{m}} = x\]

Worked Example 1: Factorise and Cancel Before Rearranging

\[y = \frac{x^2 + 6x + 9}{x+3}\]

Make \(x\) the subject.

Factorise the numerator:
\(x^2 + 6x + 9 = (x+3)^2\)
Substitute into the fraction: \[y = \frac{(x+3)^2}{x+3}\]
Cancel one \((x+3)\): \[y = x + 3 \quad (x \neq -3)\]
Subtract 3 from both sides: \[x = y - 3\]

Worked Example 2: Complete the Square to Make x the Subject

\[y = \frac{2x^2 + 12x}{4}\]

Make \(x\) the subject.

Simplify first: \[y = \frac{x^2 + 6x}{2}\]
Multiply both sides by 2: \[2y = x^2 + 6x\]
Complete the square without moving \(y\) to the \(x\)-side: \[x^2 + 6x + 9 = 2y + 9\] \[(x+3)^2 = 2y + 9\]
Square root both sides: \[x+3 = \pm\sqrt{2y+9}\]

\[x = -3 \pm \sqrt{2y+9}\]

Worked Example 3: Rearranging a Fractional Quadratic Formula

\[y = \frac{\frac{2x^2}{3} + 4x + 6}{2}\]

Make \(x\) the subject.

Multiply both sides by 2: \[2y = \frac{2x^2}{3} + 4x + 6\]
Multiply both sides by 3: \[6y = 2x^2 + 12x + 18\]
Divide both sides by 2: \[3y = x^2 + 6x + 9\]
Recognise perfect square: \[(x+3)^2 = 3y\]
Square root both sides: \[x+3 = \pm\sqrt{3y}\]

\[x = -3 \pm \sqrt{3y}\]

Worked Example 4: Negative Indices in Rearranging

\[y = 3x^{-2} + 1\]

Make \(x\) the subject.

Subtract 1 and divide by 3: \[\frac{y-1}{3} = x^{-2}\]
Index rule used: \(x^{-n} = \frac{1}{x^n}\).
So \(x^{-2} = \frac{1}{x^2}\), giving: \[\frac{y-1}{3} = \frac{1}{x^2}\]
Invert both sides: \[x^2 = \frac{3}{y-1}\]

\[x = \pm\sqrt{\frac{3}{y-1}}\]

Worked Example 5: Fractional Indices and Power Rules

\[y = 2x^{3/2} - 4\]

Make \(x\) the subject.

Add 4 and divide by 2: \[\frac{y+4}{2} = x^{3/2}\]
Raise both sides to the power \(\frac{2}{3}\): \[\left(\frac{y+4}{2}\right)^{2/3} = \left(x^{3/2}\right)^{2/3}\]
Index rule used: \((x^m)^n = x^{mn}\).
Right side becomes \(x^{(3/2)(2/3)} = x^1 = x\).

\[x = \left(\frac{y+4}{2}\right)^{2/3}\]

🎯 How to Spot "Complete the Square" Strategy

Look for this pattern: \(y = x^2 + bx\) (no constant term on the right side)

Why it works here: When there's no constant added to the \(x\) terms, completing the square is clean and direct. You don't need to rearrange \(y\) separately first.

Remember: If you see \(x^2 + \text{(something with } x \text{)} \) with no number by itself, think "complete the square immediately" — it's often the quickest path.

Question 1

Make \(x\) the subject:

\[y = x^2 + 7x\]

Complete the square directly:

\[x^2 + 7x + \left(\frac{7}{2}\right)^2 = y + \left(\frac{7}{2}\right)^2\]

\[\left(x+\frac{7}{2}\right)^2 = y + \frac{49}{4}\]

\[x+\frac{7}{2} = \pm\sqrt{y + \frac{49}{4}}\]

\[x = -\frac{7}{2} \pm \sqrt{y + \frac{49}{4}}\]

Question 2

Make \(x\) the subject:

\[y = \frac{x^2 - 9}{x + 3}\]

Factorise numerator: \(x^2 - 9 = (x-3)(x+3)\)
So \(y = \frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x+3} = x-3\), for \(x \neq -3\)

\[x = y + 3\]

Question 3

Make \(x\) the subject:

\[y = \frac{2x^2 + 12x}{4}\]

Simplify then multiply by 2: \(2y = x^2 + 6x\)
Complete the square: \((x+3)^2 = 2y+9\)
Square root both sides:

\[x = -3 \pm \sqrt{2y+9}\]

Question 4

Make \(x\) the subject:

\[y = 5x^{-3}\]

Index rule used: \(x^{-n} = \frac{1}{x^n}\)
\(\frac{y}{5} = x^{-3} = \frac{1}{x^3}\)
Invert: \(x^3 = \frac{5}{y}\)

\[x = \sqrt[3]{\frac{5}{y}}\]

Question 5

Make \(x\) the subject:

\[y = 4x^{5/2} - 1\]

\(\frac{y+1}{4} = x^{5/2}\)
Index rule used: \((x^m)^n = x^{mn}\)
Raise both sides to \(\frac{2}{5}\):

\[x = \left(\frac{y+1}{4}\right)^{2/5}\]

Question 6 (Hard)

Make \(x\) the subject:

\[y = x^{1/2} + x^{-1/2}\]

Index rule used: \(x^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{x^{1/2}}\)
Let \(u = x^{1/2}\), so \(y = u + \frac{1}{u}\)
Multiply by \(u\): \(yu = u^2 + 1\)
Quadratic in \(u\): \(u^2 - yu + 1 = 0\)
\(u = \frac{y \pm \sqrt{y^2-4}}{2}\), and \(x = u^2\)

\[x = \left(\frac{y \pm \sqrt{y^2-4}}{2}\right)^2\]

Question 7 (Hard)

Make \(x\) the subject:

\[y = \frac{x^2 + 6x + 9}{x+3}\]

\(x^2+6x+9=(x+3)^2\)
\(y=\frac{(x+3)^2}{x+3}=x+3\), with \(x\neq -3\)

\[x = y - 3\]

Question 8 (Challenging)

\(3x^2+12x+12=3(x+2)^2\)
\(y=\frac{3(x+2)^2}{x+2}=3(x+2)\), with \(x\neq -2\)
